# Agent-made changes now outrun human review

The problem is real, it is growing, and buyers are already paying to deal with it. As of late 2026, a tool that helps humans understand what an AI coding agent changed is relevant. As a standalone "architecture diff viewer" it is weak: that idea is already crowded and is turning into a free feature. The bottleneck shows up clearly in large telemetry datasets. Faros's 2026 data on 22,000 developers shows **median time in review up 441.5%**, **unreviewed merges up 31.3%** and **incidents per PR up 242.7%**. LinearB's 8.1M-PR benchmark puts the **median agentic PR at 892 lines**, far past any sensible line-by-line review limit. Money is following the problem. CodeRabbit raised at a **$1.5B valuation in August 2026**, and Tracxn counts **351 code-review startups**. But the specific visual concept has already been taken up by others. CodeRabbit's Change Stack, Devin Review, Greptile's per-PR diagrams, a YC W26 company (Whiteboard) and at least five open-source tools ship some form of "visual change review" today, and the first generation of codebase-map companies (CodeSee, Sourcetrail) died because nobody would pay. The defensible gap is narrower and harder. It combines three things: deterministic (not LLM-drawn) contract and dependency deltas; intent-vs-outcome checks that link the agent's task and session to what actually changed; and a risk-routing layer that decides which human-reviewed PRs need human eyes and where to look. Sell it to engineering leadership as merge-gate governance, not to individual developers as "understanding." The verdict is **relevant as a problem, risky as a product, and viable only as a grounded governance wedge**.

## The review bottleneck went from slow to skipped in one year

The strongest demand evidence is the change between 2025 and 2026. In Faros's 2025 report, high-AI-adoption teams merged 98% more PRs while **PR size rose 154% and review time 91%**, and bugs per developer rose 9% ([Faros 2025](https://www.faros.ai/blog/ai-productivity-paradox-report-2025)). A year later, Faros compared each organization's lowest- and highest-adoption periods across 22,000 developers. Median time to first review rose **156.6%** and median time in review **441.5%**. Meanwhile **PRs merged with no review at all rose 31.3%**, bugs per developer rose **54%**, monthly incidents rose **57.9%** and code churn rose 861% ([Faros 2026](https://faros.ai/blog/ai-acceleration-whiplash-takeaways)). In other words, reviewers first took longer, then gave up. LinearB's 2026 benchmarks show the same pattern from another angle. Agentic PRs wait about **5.3x longer to be picked up**, are then reviewed about twice as fast, and only **32.7% of AI-generated PRs merge within 30 days**, against 84.4% for manual ones ([LinearB](https://linearb.io/resources/engineering-benchmarks-report)). Long avoidance, then quick skimming, then rejection: that is what reviewers do when a change is too big to understand.

Surveys explain why. Sonar's January 2026 survey found **96% of developers don't fully trust that AI code is correct, but only 48% always check it** before committing. It also found **38% say AI code takes more effort to review** than human code ([Sonar](https://www.sonarsource.com/company/press-releases/sonar-data-reveals-critical-verification-gap-in-ai-coding/)). Stack Overflow's 2025 survey recorded distrust of AI accuracy rising to 46% ([TechRepublic](https://www.techrepublic.com/article/news-developer-trust-in-ai-declines/)). A 52-engineer study cited by Addy Osmani found the AI-assisted group scored **17% lower on a comprehension quiz** despite finishing in about the same time ([Osmani](https://addyosmani.com/blog/comprehension-debt/)). GitClear measured that moved (refactored) code fell to **3.8% of changed lines in 2026**, from 21% in 2022, while duplicated blocks grew ([The New Stack](https://thenewstack.io/ai-coding-duplication-rose/)). Codebases are growing by accretion, which makes them structurally harder to follow.

The framing has also gone mainstream, which matters for selling. Google Cloud's Office of the CTO wrote that the bottleneck "moved from the code to the people reviewing it." Its proposed fix, an AI-generated change summary plus risk assessment on every PR, is close to this product concept ([Google Cloud](https://cloud.google.com/transform/when-ai-writes-the-code-who-reviews-it-cto-google-cloud)). GitHub rebuilt its diff surface to handle a single PR with 2,200 files and over a million changed lines, and shipped stacked PRs in July 2026 in response to oversized AI PRs ([InfoQ](https://infoq.com/news/2026/11/github-pull-requests-rebuild)). "Comprehension debt" is the gap between how much code exists and how much anyone understands, and it is now a named concept in O'Reilly and the trade press ([O'Reilly](https://www.oreilly.com/radar/comprehension-debt-the-hidden-cost-of-ai-generated-code/)).

These numbers come with two caveats. First, almost every one comes from a vendor that sells into this market: Faros, LinearB, Sonar, GitClear and CodeRabbit all do, and The Register called Sonar's finding self-serving ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2026/01/09/devs_ai_code/)). Second, all of it is correlational. No independent study measures the time humans spend *understanding* agent changes, as opposed to time waiting for review. The direction is consistent across sources, but no single magnitude should be treated as precise.

## Capital is flowing to automated review, not to human comprehension

Buyers clearly pay to manage AI-generated code. CodeRabbit raised **$143M at a $1.5B valuation** in August 2026, with revenue reportedly up more than 5x year over year ([TNW](https://thenextweb.com/news/coderabbit-raised-143m-to-read-the-code-ai-wrote)). A secondary source claims it passed **$100M ARR** in November 2026, though other estimates differ sharply, so treat revenue as unverified ([ARR.club](https://www.arr.club/research/how-coderabbit-went-from-10m-to-100m-arr)). Qodo raised a $70M Series B ([Calcalist](https://www.calcalistech.com/ctechnews/article/r1qdnboswx)). Greptile raised a $25M Series A from Benchmark, and a later $50M Series B at $750M is reported but rests on thin sourcing ([aicodereview.cc](https://aicodereview.cc/blog/state-of-ai-code-review-q4-2026/)). Consolidation is fast. Cursor bought Graphite ([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2025/12/19/cursor-acquires-ai-code-review-startup-graphite/)). According to the same Q4 2026 roundup, Harness bought DeepSource and Augment assets, Sonar bought Gitar and Tricentis bought Tabnine. The roundup also counts **351 automated code-review startups, 85 of them funded**.

Almost all of that money buys *automated issue-finding*: comment bots that post findings on PRs. Pricing is moving from seats to per-review usage and getting cheaper. Cursor Bugbot dropped its $40 seat fee for about **$1.00-1.50 per run** ([repowise](https://repowise.dev/blog/comparisons/cursor-bugbot-pricing-alternatives)), Greptile charges about $1 per review, and Macroscope about $0.95 ([aicodereview.cc](https://aicodereview.cc/blog/state-of-ai-code-review-q4-2026/)). Benchmarks are disputed: Greptile claims an 82% bug catch rate and an Augment re-run found 45%. No commercial reviewer has published an independent false-positive rate. That leaves an opening. The bots do not produce human comprehension, and vendors still keep humans as the merge gate. Anthropic's Code Review, GA since October 2026, "never approves or blocks a PR" ([TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/pro/anthropic-launches-a-new-code-review-tool-to-check-ai-generated-content-but-it-might-cost-you-more-than-youd-hope)). The evidence does *not* show that anyone pays separately for visual comprehension. No data exists on how often reviewers use the diagrams already on offer.

## Visual change review is already crowded and turning into a free feature

The most direct threat is **CodeRabbit Change Stack**. Its pitch is the user's thesis almost word for word: replace the alphabetical file list with a reading order plus diagrams. It submits GitHub reviews from inside the interface, it is free "for a limited time," and it comes from the best-funded independent vendor. Its homepage already advertises "semantic diffs, blast radius context, and architectural diagrams" ([coderabbit.ai](https://www.coderabbit.ai/)). **Devin Review** gives a similar regrouping away for free with no signup. **Whiteboard** (YC W26, MIT-licensed, 2.8k stars) is building "Trace Archaeology" to link code to the agent session that wrote it, which overlaps directly with intent-vs-outcome, although that feature is unshipped. GitHub so far has only optimized the diff itself (compact diffs, million-line rendering, stacked PRs) and shipped no semantic view. It owns the surface and could add one.

## Visualization alone has failed before, and the reasons still apply

History is the biggest counter-argument. Sourcetrail was discontinued in 2021 because there were "not enough paid customers" ([InfoWorld](https://www.infoworld.com/article/2264662/sourcetrail-code-navigator-now-free-open-source.html)). CodeSee pitched "pull request diagrams" for continuous understanding back in 2021 ([The New Stack](https://thenewstack.io/codesee-helps-developers-understand-the-codebase/)), essentially this idea before agents existed. It was folded into GitKraken in 2024 ([GitKraken](https://gitkraken.com/press/gitkraken-acquires-codesee-launches-devex-platform)). Swimm has raised nothing since its 2021 Series A and reportedly moved toward services ([YesPress](https://yespress.io/swimm.md)). Academic surveys blame the low adoption of software visualization on "unproved effectiveness" ([Merino et al.](https://boris.unibe.ch/126936/8/Meri18a-postprint.pdf)) and on poor integration into existing toolchains ([EuroVAST](https://webspace.science.uu.nl/~telea001/uploads/PAPERS/EuroVAST10/paper.pdf)). The one durable independent, CodeScene, sells risk and code-health metrics to managers rather than understanding to developers ([CodeScene](https://codescene.com/pricing)). Meanwhile, a generic repo diagram is now free: **GitDiagram has 19.1k stars**, and DeepWiki is free for public repos ([Latent Space](https://latent.space/wiki/cognition-6249c527/)).

There are three further counter-arguments worth taking seriously. First, review is being automated away for low-risk changes. GitHub now lets Copilot approvals count toward branch protection, and Rewind lets AI approve PRs graded as low risk ([Rewind](https://rewind.com/blog/ai-approve-pull-requests-safely/)). Second, a credible camp prefers verification to comprehension. Google's CTO office recommends a "Conditional LGTM" tied to passing tests and describes agent code as disposable ([Google Cloud](https://cloud.google.com/transform/when-ai-writes-the-code-who-reviews-it-cto-google-cloud)). Third, LLM-drawn diagrams are unreliable. Whiteboard itself warns users that its diagrams are claims to verify ([Better Stack](https://betterstack.com/community/guides/ai/whiteboard-ai-code/)), and one developer argues call graphs are the wrong abstraction for AI code altogether ([DEV](https://dev.to/kakacheaper/i-deleted-4080-lines-and-rebuilt-from-scratch-heres-why-call-graphs-are-wrong-for-ai-generated-1606)).

The steelman for the idea survives these objections, but only in a specific form. Tests and AI reviewers do not fix comprehension debt, which "lives in people" ([O'Reilly](https://www.oreilly.com/radar/comprehension-debt-the-hidden-cost-of-ai-generated-code/)). In the c-CRAB benchmark the best review agent caught only **41.5%** of issues ([aicodereview.cc](https://aicodereview.cc/blog/state-of-ai-code-review-q4-2026/)). Incidents rising alongside automated review show current verification falling short. Automating low-risk approvals does not remove human review either. It concentrates human attention on the riskier PRs and raises the stakes of each one, so a tool that makes those few reviews fast and trustworthy becomes more valuable, not less. The best direct evidence is Columbia's **Rasoi** study (Nov 2026). In a one-week deployment with 12 developers, architectural diffs of agent edits helped people check scope and catch unexpected restructuring. Its main finding was that the value lies in **developers' confidence in their own judgment, not in trusting the agent** ([arXiv 2611.16293](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16293)). The sample is small, and no study yet shows that visual summaries speed up review or improve defect detection compared with diffs. That remains the key unvalidated assumption.

## The defensible wedge is grounded deltas plus intent checks at the merge gate

Across all three research areas, the same slots are still empty. No shipping product combines a **persistent, deterministic model of the system** with the change overlaid as a before/after delta, **contract and API surface changes** (signatures, schemas, endpoints, events, migrations) as first-class objects, **dependency additions with transitive blast radius**, and a **risk heatmap** placed over that map. Above all, no reviewer checks **intent against outcome**: whether what changed matches what the agent was asked to do, including scope creep, files nobody asked it to touch, removed functionality and architectural drift. Entire captures provenance without visualizing impact. CodeRabbit and Whiteboard visualize changes with weak provenance. Transcript replayers show the session but not the code impact ([Entire](https://entire.io/blog/hello-entire-world); [claude-replay](https://es617.github.io/claude-replay)).

That points to a recommended positioning: **"the merge-gate risk and scope layer for agent-written code."** Do not position it as "a prettier diff" or "a codebase map." In practice that means five things. First, build every structural claim from static analysis (import and call graphs, OpenAPI/proto/GraphQL schemas, DB migrations, dependency manifests) so reviewers can trust the picture, with the LLM limited to labeling and summarizing. This is the clearest contrast with Mermaid-from-a-prompt competitors, and it matches Rasoi's finding that reviewer self-confidence is the product. Second, ingest the agent's task and session (Claude Code JSONL, Entire Checkpoints, the issue or plan) and flag differences between intent and outcome. This is the least-occupied slot and the hardest for diff-centric bots to copy. Third, aggregate signals into a risk heatmap that decides *which* PRs need a human and *where* in each PR to look: blast radius, contract breaks, coverage change, security-sensitive zones and churn, plus findings from CodeRabbit, Copilot or Bugbot used as inputs rather than as rivals. That complements GitHub's push toward AI approvals instead of fighting it. Fourth, live inside the PR (a check plus a linked view) and the agent loop, because standalone viewers have historically gone unused. Fifth, sell to engineering leadership and platform teams on governance outcomes such as fewer rubber-stamped merges, contract-break prevention and auditability of agent changes. That is the buyer CodeScene and CodeRabbit monetize, and it suits the industry's shift away from seat pricing.

The risks remain substantial. CodeRabbit can add architecture views and risk flags to Change Stack quickly. GitHub owns the PR surface. Devin Review sets the price expectation at zero. A per-PR visual is easy to copy, so defensibility has to come from the change-graph engine, multi-language contract extraction and a risk model calibrated on outcomes (which flagged PRs later caused incidents). A realistic exit is acquisition by a review or platform company. Cursor, Harness, Sonar and Tricentis have all bought in this space within the past year.

## Conclusion

The question has changed since CodeSee's era. Then, visualizing changes was a nice-to-have. Now, agentic PRs average about 900 lines and unreviewed merges are climbing, so humans lack the bandwidth to understand what they approve, and that is a painkiller-grade problem. But the obvious form of the answer, a diagram of the PR, became a commodity within 2026, and incumbents will give it away. The opportunity is not to show humans the change. It is to tell them, with verifiable evidence, **whether the change is the one they asked for and whether it is safe to merge without reading it**. That reframes the product from visualization to verification of scope and contracts, with visualization as the interface.

Three things should be tested before building heavily. First, that grounded contract and dependency deltas measurably cut time to a confident approval compared with Change Stack or Devin Review on real agent PRs. Second, that intent-vs-outcome flags catch problems the comment bots miss. Third, that an engineering leader will pay for risk routing separately from the AI reviewer they already license. If those hold, the idea is relevant and defensible. If the first fails, it is a feature that CodeRabbit will ship next quarter.
